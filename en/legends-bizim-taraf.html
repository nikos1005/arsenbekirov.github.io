Bizim Taraf

How a Cheerful Song Became an Anthem of Longing for Home
Home → Song Stories → Bizim Taraf

Song Stories · Legends of Crimean Tatar Folk Songs · Research by Arsen Bekirov

A song the people consider their own

There is a highest accolade for a song — when the people forget it has an author. Bizim Taraf — "Our Land," "Our Home" — is exactly such a song. During the years of deportation, this melody became the unofficial anthem of the Crimean Tatars in exile; it was sung in every home, at every celebration, and almost no one thought to ask where it came from. Many today still believe it is a folk song.

But Bizim Taraf has authors. And not just one pair, but two generations of authors — because this song lived two entirely different lives. And the second life, the one the whole people knows today, is the work of Server Kakura.

First life: a paradise land and dancing (1966)

The story begins in 1966. Composer Turgut Shinikarov and poet Ali Saidov create the song "Jennet yery — bizim taraf" — "Paradise land, our home." It is an anthem of love for the native land, full of warmth and light. The melody is like warm sunshine, the lyrics like a letter home.

The song is unusual in form: strophic, without a refrain — a story that flows like a river. It was recorded in two versions simultaneously: instrumental — for dancing, and vocal — for the soul. Bizim Taraf was released on gramophone records, and they scattered across the world — including to Uzbekistan, where deported Crimean Tatars lived in the 1960s.

There, the legendary Kaitarma ensemble picked up the song and carried it across the cities of Uzbekistan — Tashkent, Samarkand, Bukhara. Cheerful, dance-like, sunny. But it was precisely this sunniness that became the turning point.

Second life: Tashkent, 1980

Let us transport ourselves to Tashkent, July 1980. Server Kakura, a student at the Tashkent Conservatory — a young musician studying folklore, taking his first steps as a composer. His elder brother Yunus Kakura, a mature vocalist who sang at Crimean Tatar weddings, comes to him: "Server, have you heard this song?" — and hums Bizim Taraf.

And here the brothers grasp the essential truth: this melody, all about happiness and dancing, sounds dissonant with the mood of the people in exile. Crimea is far away, longing tears at the heart — and the song calls you to dance. And they decide to rebuild it from scratch.

Yunus, as a poet, gives birth to new words: "Qara denyzylynnda — tatarlaryn vyetany bar..." — "By the Black Sea — the homeland of the Tatars..." Tenderness, pain, longing for the homeland. And Server takes on the music: he writes an introduction, adds a refrain, slows the melody, makes it deep and sorrowful — so that every chord cries out: "We are pining for our homeland."

It is this, the second version — with music by Server Kakura and words by Yunus Kakura — that is the Bizim Taraf the whole people knows and sings today. The first to perform it were the brothers themselves, Yunus aga and Server aga. A song that had recently been for dancing became an anthem of longing for the native land.

I know this story not from hearsay: Server Kakura is the artistic director of the ensemble in which I sing, and I heard from him personally how the second life of Bizim Taraf was born. That is why I can tell it here as it actually happened — firsthand.

Coming home (1989)

In 1989, upon the people's return to Crimea, Bizim Taraf was heard on native soil for the first time — performed by Alim Osmanov and the Kara Deniz ensemble on Crimean state television. And then the real explosion began: the song definitively became part of the people's soul.

Everyone sang it. Rustem Memetov, the rock group Merjan in the 1990s with their own version, Elzara Batolova — with such tenderness that tears well up. And later, the world star Jamala. Each performance is a new shade of the same feeling. It went so far that some were ready to claim authorship for themselves — and that is the best indicator that the song struck the heart precisely.

Why it matters

Bizim Taraf has long been considered a folk song — and that is the highest praise for a song: it means it struck the heart and settled in the soul. But behind this "folk" melody lies an extraordinary journey: from a sunny dance song of 1966 — through Tashkent exile, where Server and Yunus Kakura rebuilt it into a song of longing for the homeland — to a symbol of coming home. To return to the song its true authors and its true history — that is the purpose of the Song Stories series.

Bizim Taraf is the second song in the Song Stories series. Here I collect the histories of Crimean Tatar folk songs — where they come from, how they changed, and what lies behind a familiar melody. To be continued.
