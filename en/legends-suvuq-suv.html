Suvuq Suv

The Name They Wouldn't Let Be Forgotten
Home → Song Stories → Suvuq Suv

Song Stories · Legends of Crimean Tatar Folk Songs · Research by Arsen Bekirov

A village that remembers

There is a place in the southeastern foothills of Crimea that bore the same name for almost three centuries — and lost it in a single night. The village was called Suvuq Suv — "Cold Water" — after the stream that flows here with spring water, icy even in the height of summer.

The village is far older than it appears: archaeologists have found remains of pottery and bronze-casting workshops dating to the 8th–10th centuries. The traveller Evliya Çelebi, passing through in 1667, recorded in his Book of Travels: "This is a village of two hundred houses, one mosque, one bath, and one inn." The mosque is documented in an 1864 registry, and a Tatar school appears in the 1926 census.

A land of tobacco growers, not grain farmers

The southeastern foothills of Crimea were for centuries a region of tobacco cultivation, viticulture, and orchards — not breadfields. When a terrible famine struck Crimea in the early 1920s, these tobacco-growing areas were devastated catastrophically: the trade network collapsed, and there was nothing to feed on. In neighbouring villages of the same cluster, Elbuzly and Taraktash, the death of a significant portion of the population in a single winter is documented. Suvuq Suv belonged to the same economic pattern and the same region.

May 18, 1944

The main blow came on May 18, 1944. According to deportation documents, 209 people — 30 Crimean Tatar families — were to be expelled from Suvuq Suv. They had fifteen minutes to pack. Fifteen minutes to leave a home that had stood for three hundred years, a garden, a spring, the graves of ancestors.

By decree of the Presidium of the Supreme Soviet on May 18, 1948, hundreds of Crimean names were replaced with new ones — and so Suvuq Suv became Lesnoy. A name that had lived for nearly three centuries since its first written mention vanished from official maps.

A song that begins with the name

This is exactly what the song is about. It begins with the name itself: "Suvuqsuvda aytivli dostlar" — "friends, let us speak of Suvuq Suv." And the key word here is not "remember," not "mourn." It is "speak," "say aloud." The very act of naming is the song's content.

The third verse describes the moment of loading onto the cattle trucks — by that point the village's name has been repeated so many times that it has become not just a place but a sound the body makes when the last thing it can hold on to is taken away.

Today

Today about 600 people live in the village; in the last census nearly a quarter of residents named Crimean Tatar as their native language — those who returned after the collapse of the USSR to a place that no longer has its native name on any official map.
